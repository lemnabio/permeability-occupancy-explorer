The model, and what it assumes

The cell is one well-mixed compartment behind one membrane. Flux is passive and proportional to the concentration difference in free drug.

k = Pe / (V/A)cell rate constant, 1/s τ = 1 / (k · ER) time constant, unbuffered dL/dt = k · (Cmed,free − ER · Lfree) − kdeg · L L = Lfree/fu,cell + [T] · Lfree/(Lfree + KD) occupancy = Lfree / (Lfree + KD)

L is total drug inside, L_free is what the target sees. Medium is a fixed reservoir decaying only by the stability half-life.

Three consequences worth arguing about with the bench:

1. Permeability sets the rate, not the endpoint. Without efflux, steady-state free cytosol equals free medium whatever logPe is. Below about −8 the equilibration time finally exceeds a working day, and that is the only reason a low logPe kills a plate experiment.

2. Efflux is different in kind from every other loss. It is the one mechanism that makes cytosol lower than medium at steady state rather than just slower to get there. It also speeds equilibration, because it adds to the total rate of leaving.

3. Target abundance does not change steady-state occupancy. It buffers the approach, so a high-abundance target takes longer to load, but it lands in the same place. Abundance only caps you if the wells run out of compound, which the mass-balance line checks.

Not modelled: active uptake, endocytosis and endosomal entrapment, lysosomal trapping, pH partitioning, membrane potential opposing a charged species, intracellular metabolism, or binding kinetics slower than transport. Endosomal uptake is the important omission for live-cell PELSA, since it produces cell-associated compound that engages target only after lysis.
